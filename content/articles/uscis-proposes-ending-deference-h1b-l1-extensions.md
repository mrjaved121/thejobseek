The US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has proposed a rule that could make it harder to extend some US work visas. The proposal, titled **"Removing Deference to Prior Determinations of Eligibility,"** was filed for public inspection on **13 October 2026** and scheduled for publication in the Federal Register on 14 October.

**This is a proposal, not a final rule.** Current rules still apply.

## What "deference" means

Today, when an employer asks to **extend** a worker's visa for the **same job, with the same employer and the same facts**, USCIS officers generally defer to their earlier approval. They only look again if there was a material error, a material change, or new information that affects eligibility.

The new rule would remove that approach. Officers would review **every extension on its own merits**, as if it were a new case. A past approval would no longer automatically support a new one.

## Which visas are affected

The proposal covers extension petitions filed on Form I-129, including:

- **H-1B** (specialty occupations)
- **L-1** (intra-company transfers)
- **O-1**, **TN**, **E**, **H-2**, **P**, **Q** and **R** categories

## What it could mean for workers

According to reporting on the proposal, DHS expects:

- **more Requests for Evidence (RFEs)** on extensions, potentially thousands more each year
- **more documents** needed from employers
- **longer processing** for some cases

DHS says the change would strengthen fraud detection, avoid repeating past mistakes and protect US workers.

## A policy that keeps changing

Deference was first adopted in 2004, withdrawn in 2017, brought back in 2021 and written into the H-1B modernisation rule in December 2024. This proposal would reverse it again.

## What to do now

- **No action is needed yet** if your extension is not due. The rule is not in force.
- If you are on an H-1B or L-1 and your extension is coming up, ask your employer to **prepare a complete file** rather than relying on the previous approval.
- The public can comment on the proposal during the comment period, which was reported as 30 days. Check the Federal Register page for the exact deadline.
